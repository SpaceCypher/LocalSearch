# Compressed inverted index beats the fingerprint prototype

Build a conventional compressed, memory-mapped inverted index, not the folder-clustered fingerprint index. The prototype's 29.6 MB works out to about 33 bits per term-document pair, which is four to five times what compressed inverted indexes achieve on published benchmarks, and it only looks good because the 172 MB baseline is an uncompressed hash-map structure at about 192 bits per pair. A plain array of 16-bit document id, 8-bit term frequency and 8-bit field mask would already take 28.6 MB with exact BM25, and a Lucene/Tantivy-style layout is estimated at roughly 15 to 26 MB on disk including the term dictionary, with only the pages a query touches resident. Those inverted-index sizes are estimates derived from published codec figures and layout arithmetic, not measurements on this corpus, so the first step is to measure them. The prototype's building blocks are established prior art (BitFunnel, Bloofi, COBS, RAMBO, Bloomier filters); its specific combination and its desktop-scale measurements appear unpublished, but the systems that ship such filters use them as a pre-filter beside an inverted index or ahead of a separate ranker, never as the ranked index. Every desktop and embedded engine surveyed handles updates the same way: immutable segments, a small mutable delta, a tombstone bitset for deletes, and background merges. Folder-path ordering is worth little to a filter index (the team's 6% is consistent with the literature) but is the desktop analogue of URL ordering, which cuts bit-packed web-scale indexes by 27 to 43%; expect a smaller, low-double-digit gain here, obtained for free at each rebuild.

## The prototype's 5.8x saving is measured against an uncompressed baseline

The headline numbers reduce to simple arithmetic. The current index spends 172 MB on 7,157,948 pairs, about 24 bytes each, which signals `HashMap` and `Vec` overhead rather than information content. The prototype spends 29.6 MB, about **33 bits per pair**, and stores no usable term frequency. Because 59,642 documents fit in 16 bits, an entirely uncompressed posting of u16 document id, u8 frequency and u8 field mask costs 4 bytes, or **28.6 MB for the whole corpus with exact frequencies**, and 21.5 MB if the field mask is folded elsewhere. The prototype therefore gives up exact ranking, enumeration and deletes to roughly tie with the most naive exact layout.

Compression widens the gap. On full web-scale indexes with every term included and random document order, bit-packed and Elias-Fano-style codecs cost 7.5 to 9.4 bits per document id and 2.1 to 5.6 bits per frequency, about 1.3 to 1.8 bytes per posting; byte-aligned codecs such as StreamVByte cost about 2.8 bytes ([Mallia et al., ECIR 2019](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). These figures do not transfer directly, and the reason matters. This corpus averages 13.4 postings per term against roughly 161 to 172 in Gov2 and ClueWeb09 ([Mallia et al.](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)), and the standard codec survey discards every list shorter than 4,096 postings ([Pibiri & Venturini](https://arxiv.org/abs/1908.10598)). Short lists have wide gaps and cannot use 128-entry bit-packed blocks.

Production engines already handle this regime. Lucene and Tantivy bit-pack only full blocks of 128 and encode shorter lists and tails as variable-length integers; Lucene shifts the document delta left one bit so that a frequency of 1 costs no extra byte, and writes a singleton's document id straight into the term dictionary instead of a file pointer ([Lucene912PostingsFormat](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_0_0/core/org/apache/lucene/codecs/lucene912/Lucene912PostingsFormat.html); [Tantivy ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)). Applying that layout here, the research notes estimate **1.5 to 2.5 bytes per posting, about 11 to 18 MB of postings**. The term dictionary, which the fingerprint design avoids entirely, adds an estimated 1.5 to 5.3 MB as a finite-state transducer, scaled from the fst crate author's measurements of roughly 3 to 10 bytes per natural-language key ([BurntSushi](https://burntsushi.net/transducers/)), plus an estimated 1.6 to 2.7 MB of per-term metadata in Tantivy's bit-packed 256-term blocks ([tantivy term_info_store.rs](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/src/termdict/fst_termdict/term_info_store.rs)).

The more important difference is where the bytes live. Tantivy readers are "designed to require very little anonymous memory. The data is read straight from an mmapped file" ([Tantivy ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)), and Lucene delegates memory management to the operating system's page cache the same way ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/whats-new-in-lucene-8)). File-backed pages are evictable, so the app's own heap shrinks to a mapping, per-query decode buffers and a one-byte-per-document length array. No source gives a measured resident-set figure for a small mapped index, so that number also needs measuring. The scaling argument is decisive at one million files: document ids grow to 20 bits and common-term lists start to resemble the web-scale benchmarks, so a compressed index improves per posting, while the fingerprint index stays near 33 bits per pair and must sit in RAM. That extrapolation assumes postings grow in proportion to file count.

## Every piece of the fingerprint design has published prior art

The research record on per-document probabilistic signatures is long, and its verdict has been stable. Zobel, Moffat and Ramamohanarao concluded in 1998 that "inverted files are distinctly superior to signature files", needing less time, less space and offering more functionality ([Moffat](https://people.eng.unimelb.edu.au/ammoffat/abstracts/zmr98acmtods.html)). BitFunnel, the Bing system that revived signatures in 2017, overturned only the throughput claim: on five Gov2 shards it used **11.69 to 38.43 bits per posting against 6.15 to 7.64 for Partitioned Elias-Fano**, and it was worst on the shard of short documents with 64 to 127 unique terms ([BitFunnel, SIGIR 2017](https://danluu.com/bitfunnel-sigir.pdf)). This corpus averages about 120 distinct terms per file, squarely in that weakest regime.

The prototype's structure maps onto named techniques. A Bloom filter summarising a block of 64 files is one of BitFunnel's "higher rank rows", where one bit stands for 2^r documents; the paper warns that such rows "magnify the bit density" and add correlated noise ([BitFunnel](https://danluu.com/bitfunnel-sigir.pdf)). Bloofi builds the same OR-of-children hierarchy and notes it works well only for sparsely populated filters and that inserts must update inner nodes ([Bloofi](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1501.01941)). COBS sizes each filter to its document at a constant false-positive rate ([COBS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.09624)), and RAMBO merges groups of documents into shared filters across several independent groupings ([RAMBO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1910.02611)). The measured rise from 21 to 135 blocks opened per lookup after edits is therefore the expected saturation behaviour of OR-summaries, not an implementation defect.

Ranking is the telling point. BitFunnel is explicitly a matching filter placed ahead of a "ranking oracle", and the production version in Bing "includes a forward index with term frequencies used for BM25F ranking" ([BitFunnel](https://danluu.com/bitfunnel-sigir.pdf)). The team's 61% top-20 overlap without frequencies rediscovers why. The 2-bit bucket is also textbook: a fuse or xor filter is a retrieval data structure that returns an r-bit value per key, with a false-positive rate of 2^-r for the bits used as fingerprint ([BuRR](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.01892)). Splitting the existing 16-bit slot into a 14-bit fingerprint and a 2-bit bucket therefore costs no memory and moves the false-match rate from 1 in 65,536 to 1 in 16,384. That is the cheapest way to get the 86% overlap figure, but 86% is still a visible ranking regression against an exact index that is no larger.

Size is also near its floor. Binary fuse filters reach about 9 bits per key with 8-bit fingerprints, doubling for 16-bit, but only "for large sets, with at least a million keys"; the authors state that for sets under 20,000 keys the benefits shrink ([Graf & Lemire](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.01174)). The notes' arithmetic puts the theoretical floor for the per-file filters at about 16 MB, so even a perfectly tuned version lands where the compressed inverted index is estimated to be, without frequencies, a dictionary, prefix search or enumeration.

What appears to be new is narrow. No publication was found that uses xor or binary fuse filters as the per-document signature in a text index, that evaluates ranking quality against quantised frequency bits inside such a structure, that measures block-summary decay under edits, or that reports any of this at desktop scale. This is absence of evidence from one literature search, not proof of novelty. The practical precedent points the other way: the Rust code-search tool `ix` runs a trigram inverted index first and uses 256-byte per-file Bloom filters only as a second-stage candidate filter ([moeix README](https://docs.rs/crate/moeix/0.12.9/source/README.md)), and a 2019 follow-up to BitFunnel hybridised it with Elias-Fano lists rather than replacing them ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/pdf/10.1145/3308558.3313553)). One structural cost deserves emphasis: key-free filters cannot enumerate their terms, so repairing a stale block summary requires re-tokenising its 63 unchanged files or storing their term lists separately, which is the forward index the design was meant to avoid.

## Shipping tools keep postings on disk and only a dictionary hot

No surveyed desktop or code-search tool uses per-document filters as its primary index. Spotlight stores each volume's index in `.Spotlight-V100/Store-V2/<UUID>/` as about 99 files: a proprietary metadata store plus separate dictionary, index and posting files, with recent changes held in transient posting tables that are later folded into static ones and deletions deferred until housekeeping ([Eclectic Light](https://eclecticlight.co/2025/07/30/a-deeper-dive-into-spotlight-indexes/)). Apple documents none of this; the format is known from reverse engineering, and no reliable size or memory figure exists.

The embedded libraries share one architecture and publish usable size ratios. SQLite's own measurement on 1,636 MiB of email gives an FTS5 index of **743 MiB with positions, 340 MiB with column tags only, and 134 MiB with document ids only**, that is 45%, 21% and 8% of the text ([SQLite FTS5](https://www.sqlite.org/fts5.html)). Lucene claims 20 to 30% of indexed text ([Lucene](https://lucene.apache.org/core/features.html)), Xapian "somewhat smaller than the source data" and about two-thirds of that without positions ([Xapian FAQ](https://trac.xapian.org/wiki/FAQ/DatabaseSize)), and Windows Search about 10% of content ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/troubleshoot/windows-client/shell-experience/windows-search-performance-issues)). These ratios are against extracted text, a quantity the team has not yet reported for its corpus.

Code search trades size for substring matching. Russ Cox's document-level trigram index of the 420 MB Linux kernel is 77 MB and is read through `mmap` ([Russ Cox](https://swtch.com/~rsc/regexp/regexp4.html)); Zoekt's positional trigram shards run to about 3.5 times the corpus but are laid out to be memory-mapped ([Zoekt design](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sourcegraph/zoekt/main/doc/design.md)). Sourcegraph's 2021 work is the most transferable lesson for a Rust app: on a server with 19,000 repositories it cut live heap from 22 GB to 4 GB, chiefly by replacing the in-memory hash map from trigram to posting location, at roughly 40 bytes per entry, with sorted arrays and binary search ([Sourcegraph](https://sourcegraph.com/blog/zoekt-memory-optimizations-for-sourcegraph-cloud/)). The app's 24 bytes per posting is the same disease.

The counter-examples are warnings. Everything keeps its whole database in RAM at about 100 MB per million files for names alone, and its 1.5 alpha content indexing costs roughly the size of the indexed text in RAM ([voidtools FAQ](https://www.voidtools.com/faq/); [voidtools forum](https://voidtools.com/forum/viewtopic.php?p=74571)). Baloo stores one mutable posting list per term in LMDB, so adding a document rewrites the whole entry for every term, "leading to huge IO usage" ([KDE Baloo MR 91](https://invent.kde.org/frameworks/baloo/-/merge_requests/91)). At Everything's density, filenames for 60,000 files cost well under 10 MB, so the name index can stay fully resident while content postings move to disk.

## Tombstones and a small delta replace the delete pass

Lucene, Tantivy, FTS5, Zoekt and Spotlight all refuse to edit posting lists in place. In Lucene a delete or update only marks a bit in a per-segment bitset; searches skip those documents and space returns when segments merge, and an update is simply a delete followed by an add ([Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/lucenes-handling-of-deleted-documents)). Tantivy writes a per-segment tombstone bitset at commit and swaps `meta.json` atomically ([Tantivy ARCHITECTURE.md](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/ARCHITECTURE.md)). FTS5's contentless-delete mode attaches rowid tombstones and drops them at merge, without needing the old text ([SQLite FTS5](https://www.sqlite.org/fts5.html)), which suits a file indexer because the old content is gone by the time the change event arrives. For 60,000 to one million documents the live-documents bitset is 7.5 to 125 KB and the query-time cost is one bit test per candidate.

The recommended shape is a two-level index: one immutable, path-ordered, memory-mapped base plus a small in-memory delta holding recently changed files, with a tombstone bitset masking superseded base documents. This is the "re-merge" strategy that Lester, Williams and Zobel found fastest for large numbers of updates ([CRPIT](https://crpit.scem.westernsydney.edu.au/abstracts/CRPITV26Lester.html)), and the cost of keeping the delta appendable is modest: an immediate-access dynamic index runs about 2.0 to 2.1 bytes per posting against 1.16 to 1.80 for static ones ([Moffat & Mackenzie](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.06030)). Merge triggers can be borrowed from tested defaults: FTS5 merges at four segments per level, forces a merge at sixteen, and makes a segment merge-eligible at 10% tombstones ([SQLite FTS5](https://www.sqlite.org/fts5.html)); Lucene tolerates 20% deleted documents ([Lucene TieredMergePolicy](https://lucene.apache.org/core/10_1_0/core/org/apache/lucene/index/TieredMergePolicy.html)). No source states a size below which two levels suffice; that judgement rests on the base being small enough to rewrite in about a second, and a third tier is the standard fix once it is not.

Several design points follow from the sources rather than being documented by them, and should be treated as recommendations. Cap the delta by size so that a burst such as a branch checkout flushes to an intermediate segment instead of growing the heap. Combine document-frequency and length statistics across base and delta, and store block-level score bounds as (length, frequency) pairs rather than precomputed scores, as Tantivy and Lucene do, because collection statistics shift with every edit ([tantivy skip.rs](https://github.com/quickwit-oss/tantivy/blob/main/src/postings/skip.rs); [Elastic](https://www.elastic.co/blog/faster-retrieval-of-top-hits-in-elasticsearch-with-block-max-wand)). Tantivy's skip entry is 8 bytes per 128 postings and exists only for lists of at least 128, so reserving it costs at most about 447 KB here; pruning itself can wait, since plain MaxScore is competitive with block-max methods even at web scale ([Mallia et al.](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). Keep paths in a separate mutable document table so a rename touches no content postings. For crash safety, write the new base, sync it, rename it over a manifest that records the FSEvents event id it reflects, and replay events from that id at startup; the file system is the source of truth, so no write-ahead log is needed.

## Path ordering is free in an inverted index and nearly worthless in a filter

The team's 6% result is what the literature predicts. A fuse filter's size depends only on a file's term count, so ordering can help only the block summaries. Published signature systems gain from ordering by document size, not topic: COBS cut a 1,000-document index from 16,236 MiB to 3,022 MiB by grouping similar-sized documents ([COBS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1905.09624)). No source measures path or URL ordering on a filter index for text, so the team's figure is the only data point.

Delta-coded lists behave differently. Lexicographic URL ordering improved compression by up to 40% on about six million web pages ([Silvestri, ECIR 2007](https://ir.webis.de/anthology/2007.ecir_conference-2007.12)). With a bit-packed codec, URL order shrank Gov2 by **43% and ClueWeb09 by 27%** against random order, while variable-byte methods "benefit little" and their frequency encodings not at all ([Mallia et al.](https://research.engineering.nyu.edu/~suel/papers/daat-ecir19.pdf)). Recursive graph bisection adds a further 13 to 23% over natural order on web collections ([Dhulipala et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1602.08820)), and Lucene's implementation cut Wikipedia postings by 18.8% and sped up some conjunctive queries by 64% while slowing some disjunctions by 3.5% ([Lucene PR 12489](https://github.com/apache/lucene/pull/12489)).

Three facts discount those numbers for this app. Most lists here are too short for bit-packing and will sit in the variable-length tail path, which gains least. Lucene's reorderer considers only terms appearing in at least 4,096 documents ([Lucene BPIndexReorderer](https://lucene.apache.org/core/9_9_1/misc/org/apache/lucene/misc/index/BPIndexReorderer.html)), a threshold few terms reach at 60,000 files. And the same Lucene benchmark on ten million short documents showed postings moving only from 1,706 to 1,685 MB ([Lucene PR 12489](https://github.com/apache/lucene/pull/12489)). The notes' estimate is a low-double-digit percentage reduction in document-id bits from path order, concentrated in common terms and growing toward one million files, with little extra from graph bisection. No published measurement exists for directory-ordered file-system or source-code corpora.

Maintaining the order costs nothing in the recommended design. Lucene's sorting merge policy leaves freshly flushed segments in arrival order and sorts only merged ones ([Lucene SortingMergePolicy](https://lucene.apache.org/core/5_3_0/misc/org/apache/lucene/index/SortingMergePolicy.html)); likewise the delta stays unordered and each base rebuild reassigns ids in sorted-path order. Document ids then change across rebuilds, so everything outside the index must refer to files by a stable key.

## Conclusion

The prototype answered a question worth asking, and its answer is that the memory problem was never about index theory: it is 24 bytes of container overhead per posting. Any disciplined layout removes that, and the exact one is no larger than the approximate one. The fingerprint work retains value in two places: as unpublished measurements (ranking overlap against frequency bits, summary decay under edits), and possibly as a small secondary filter, though at 2 to 13 ms per query on 60,000 files nothing currently needs filtering.

The remaining uncertainty is empirical and cheap to remove. Three measurements settle the design: index the corpus with Tantivy (MIT, 0.26.2, released September 2026 per [crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/tantivy)) and read the term and postings file sizes and the resident set under query load; record the total extracted text so the FTS5 and Lucene ratios can be applied; and encode delta-coded postings under path order and arbitrary order to replace the ordering estimate with a number. If Tantivy's segment model and dependency weight are acceptable, adopt it. If not, the same format is assemblable from maintained crates: `fst` for the dictionary, `bitpacking` for full blocks, hand-written variable-length integers for tails, and `memmap2` for mapping ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/bitpacking)). A contentless FTS5 table through SQLite is the lowest-effort fallback at 8 to 21% of text with BM25 built in, at the price of phrase queries.
